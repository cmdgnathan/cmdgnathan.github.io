Using Music to Learn about Learning

LLMs (like ChatGPT and Gemini) have taken the world by storm. Data centers are being built, ponds are being drained, all for the sake of building and serving the next model. Frontier labs have spent an enormous amount of money and resources. Is AI research only accessible to the rich and powerful?

In this blog, I argue that AI research is much more accessible than we think. With my trusty 3 year old GPU (Nvidia 3060), I will turn to music. Music is the perfect sandbox for exploring AI, from pretraining, to supervised fine tuning, RL, and even reasoning.

Fundamentally, music is a compact form of expression, with a much simpler vocabulary than text. Think about it, with just 12 notes (in the Western scale), composers have wrought melodies that stand the test of time, with both elegant simplicity and depth of meaning. In this post, we will work with music. We will see how far we can extend the analogy of ChatGPT’s next word prediction, to a musical next note predictor.

Music is a greattractablecompactefficientfastintuitivefun playground for exploring AI.

  1. 1

    Small vocabulary. Most musical expression can be described by 12 notes (the western scale). Once you factor in Bar, Position, Pitch, Velocity, and Duration tokens, that’s a vocabulary of roughly 564 tokens — against GPT-2’s 50,257-token, byte-level BPE vocabulary.

  2. 2

    Compact datasets. All of pre-1800 classical music fits in about 84MB, roughly 14.8M tokens — against GPT-2’s own training set, WebText: about 40GB of text from roughly 8 million Reddit-linked documents, an estimated 9–10B tokens.

  3. 3

    Runs on consumer hardware in hours. Smaller vocabularies and smaller datasets mean the whole pipeline — pretraining through fine-tuning — fits on hardware you can actually own. For comparison: GPT-2’s original 1.5B-parameter model trained on 32 TPU v3 chips for about a week, an estimated $43,000 in compute — OpenAI never disclosed the actual cost.

  4. 4

    Preference is intuitive. ChatGPT leans on preference fine-tuning to steer its outputs. For music, “I like this better” is an immediate, gut-level judgment — no domain expertise required.

  5. 5

    The outputs are fun. Honestly, reason enough on its own.

Illustrative tones, not model audio — no real standalone 3-note clip exists in this project’s corpus.

In this blog, we will explore the following topics:

  1. 1

    We will use a General Pretrained Transformer to produce a probability distribution of next “note” predictions, instead of next “token” predictions. This distribution will be learned from pre-1800 classical music.

  2. 2

    Once trained, our GPT model will be an average of all composers. We will explore Supervised Fine Tuning to specialize this model to better match individual composers. We will use these SFT variants as a proxy for long-deceased composers, asking them to finish Beethoven’s (and others’) sentences.

  3. 3

    We may not like what we hear. We will explore Preference Fine Tuning via RLHF, so that we can generate music closer to our personal tastes.

  4. 4

    The above training techniques generate “meandering” music. We will address this shortcoming with Reasoning. We will also explore the concept of “Slow Thinking and Fast Thinking”, where a model reasons separately about chords and melodies.

Key findings

The LLM playbook works.

1Music vs Text

Compared to text generation, music generation is far more tractable for hobbyist hardware.

GPT-Music

4,000 BPE564 base tokens

GPT-2

50,257 BPE

Music’s raw vocabulary is inherently small — the Western scale has just 12 notes, against the tens of thousands of words and subwords a language model has to cover. That head start compounds: even after both vocabularies grow through merging, GPT-Music’s stays two orders of magnitude smaller than GPT-2’s.

BPE (byte-pair encoding) builds a vocabulary bottom-up: starting from a small base alphabet, it repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair of tokens into one new token, growing the vocabulary until it hits a target size. GPT-Music runs this over the 564-token REMI alphabet (bar, position, pitch, velocity, duration) up to 4,000 merged tokens; GPT-2 runs the same algorithm over raw bytes, all the way up to 50,257.

GPT-Music

84MB2.9K pieces

GPT-2

40GB8M docs

Text has to cover the world’s knowledge, so pretraining corpora run to tens of gigabytes of scraped documents. GPT-Music only has to cover common-practice harmony and voice-leading, so a curated few thousand classical MIDI pieces is enough to learn the style.

GPT-Music

27.8M

GPT-2

117M–1.5B

Model capacity roughly tracks the size of the vocabulary and dataset it has to fit, so a smaller vocabulary and smaller corpus can be modeled with far fewer parameters. GPT-Music’s 27.8M parameters land below even the smallest released GPT-2, and it’s still enough to capture classical style.

Training cost scales with parameters, dataset size, and vocabulary together, so shrinking all three compounds into a much smaller compute bill. The whole GPT-Music pipeline finishes on a single consumer GPU in a few hours, versus a week on a TPU pod for GPT-2.

GPT-2’s web-scraped text corpus is a mix of copyrighted material used without the authors’ permission, a legal gray area LLM makers still contend with. Beethoven, Bach, and their contemporaries have been dead for well over a century, so training on their scores raises no such questions.

2Pretraining

Pretraining gives GPT generic classical “chops”.

Pretraining is where GPT learns anything at all — one frozen base model trained once on all pre-1800 classical music, no composer identity or preference signal involved yet. The training curve itself tells a real story: early checkpoints sound sparse and undertrained, later ones sound stylistically fluent, and pushed past that point the model starts repeating itself.

What it actually learns is real but local — measurable, composer-agnostic next-note probabilities that capture the “syntax” of classical harmony, not a sense of a piece’s larger shape or destination (that comes later, if at all — see Reasoning). Vocabulary design is a real lever here too: a smaller BPE vocabulary measurably simplifies the output.

3Mid-Training

Mid-training (composer-specific SFT) gives GPT a slight “hint” of that composer — but it’s not that strong.

Mid-training fine-tunes a light, separate adapter per composer on top of the same pretrained base — a nudge, not a rewrite. The hint is real but modest: composer identity shows up in measurable next-note probabilities and in how a model’s hidden states cluster by composer, not in a dramatic stylistic transformation.

Two real longitudinal patterns run through this section: predecessors were measurably less likely than successors to choose Beethoven’s actual E♭, and note-choice entropy trends upward across the centuries — both real signs that later composers’ habits genuinely shifted, not just Beethoven’s one famous choice. Training amount matters too, though not always in the same direction for every composer.

4Post-Training

Post-training (RLVR, RLHF) improves the model without feeding it music pieces — instead, it synthesizes music for us, and we provide our preference.

No modern music was ever added to training — every attempt below tries to move the frozen classical base toward “sounds more like rock” using only RL preference signals: a reward function scores a generation, and preference optimization nudges the policy toward higher-scoring output.

The reward itself went through several real rounds, each one responding to the previous round’s specific failure: one human labeling by hand (too little volume to learn anything) → an automated classifier standing in for a human ear (more volume, but gameable) → hand-designed interpretable signals replacing the black-box classifier → those signals fused into one reward (worked, but still gameable on individual components) → a panel of weak learners replacing the single fused scorer (genuinely more stable training) → a differently-designed reward scored on non-local structure instead of local features.

Read plainly across all of it: training got more stable and the rewards got harder to game with each round, but no single round in this arc produced a clean, ungamed “this genuinely sounds like rock now” result — each fix mostly closed the previous round’s specific exploit rather than solving the underlying problem outright. See the tiles below for the real numbers behind each round.

Interpretability is exactly what let the next tile diagnose, rather than just observe, what went wrong when they were combined into one reward.

Combined, the four signals separate rock from classical well — a genuine improvement over relying on power_chord_rate alone. They weren’t actually used to train a live reward, though: validation was the deliverable here, not deployment.

The four signals were combined into one rock-vs-classical classifier (5-fold CV AUC 0.851, trained only on held-out reference features, never on the generator’s own outputs) — the single fused reward that drove the RLVR run in the previous section.

On a fixed 8-seed evaluation, the two biggest classifier-score gains turned out to be driven by chromaticism_rate and syncopation_rate collapsing, not rising. The exploit path was visible in the classifier’s own coefficients before training even started — the features were correlated enough that the optimizer could cheaply move one proxy instead of earning the real thing.

6Slow Thinking and Fast Thinking

Splitting generation into a slow System 2 planner and a fast System 1 executor — an idea borrowed from robotic manipulation research — works surprisingly well for music.

System 1 and System 2 is a classic distinction from cognitive science, popularized by Daniel Kahneman: System 1 is fast, automatic, and intuitive; System 2 is slow, deliberate, and effortful. We borrow the vocabulary loosely, not literally — mapping it onto whatever two processes in a given system run at different speeds and levels of deliberation.

The same fast/slow split, translated across three vocabularies — System 1 always executes at full speed; System 2 always sets slower-changing context.

Across the tiles below, System 2 ranges from fully static (hard-coded ii-V-I, or a one-shot plan proposed once upfront) to fully reactive (adjusting to System 1 turn by turn, or full real-time back-and-forth). One real, honest surprise from this project: the simplest static one-shot design has, on direct listening, not yet been beaten by any of the fancier reactive variants tried since.

The same fast/slow split shows up in a different guise in the Reasoning section’s own token-budget study: forcing a dedicated “System 2” to choose reasoning tokens beat letting the melody-generating “System 1” choose them for itself — the same basic finding, that separating planning from execution helps, recurring in two independent experiments.

Same System 1 checkpoint, three arms differing only in who chooses the reasoning tokens: no reasoning tokens at all, System 1 generating them for itself, or a separate System 2 specialist forcing them. Preliminary — not yet replicated at scale.

Letting the model decide its own reasoning tokens didn’t help — structural self-similarity tied the no-reasoning baseline exactly, and pitch diversity collapsed to zero. Forcing them from a separate System 2 was the only arm to beat the baseline’s repetition at all.

System 2 plans one chord per measure; System 1 generates notes conditioned on that plan. A hand-authored test fed the single most canonical progression in jazz pedagogy straight to System 1, bypassing System 2 entirely:

II_minor7 → II_minor7 → V_dominant7 → V_dominant7 → I_major7 → I_major7

Unconditioned — same model, no chord plan

Chord-conditioned — System 1 follows the ii-V-I plan

Honest measurement, not just by ear: generated notes land on the intended chord’s root/third/fifth 21–25% of the time when conditioned, versus a ~23.6% unconditioned baseline — a real but modest fidelity signal, not a dramatic one.

This is the “one-shot” baseline protocol, not the reactive turn-taking loop above: System 2 proposes the whole chord+rhythm+tempo plan ONCE, upfront, then stays out of the way while System 1 plays the entire piece straight through — no phrase-by-phrase back-and-forth.

The ii–V–I test (Slow Thinking and Fast Thinking, below) proves System 1 can follow forced jazz harmony on a hand-picked textbook progression. The more interesting listen is what happens on a real, longer, changing progression fed straight through: System 2’s own seed-9001 target plan, eight chords deep — III_major · VII_major · I_minor · VII_major · III_major · VI_major · VII_major · III_major — with System 1 (the FIM-base checkpoint, chord-conditioned via LoRA) generating the whole melody straight through.

System 1, conditioned on System 2’s real target plan — 231 real notes, 51.4s, 33 unique pitches across a 60-semitone (5-octave) range

System 1 was never trained on jazz melody — only classical. What it’s tracking here is eight real, changing chord labels handed to it by System 2, the same conditioning channel as the ii–V–I test, just on a progression it never saw shaped exactly this way.

Given a fixed total vocab budget, there’s more than one way to spend it on “planning”: grow the BPE vocabulary itself, reserve some of it for explicit reasoning tokens and let System 1 decide when to use them, or hand that same decision to a separate System 2 planner instead.

We have shown that the LLM playbook for text generation maps nicely to music generation. One might argue: music is a computational reduction of text.

GPT(music) ≤ GPT(text)

Here, a computational reduction could imply the following: if we cannot solve an issue in the music sphere, then we likely can’t solve it in the text sphere.

3-SAT ≤ Traveling Salesman

The classic complexity-theory pattern: reductions carry hardness. If I can’t discover a novelty mechanism in music, then we likely can’t discover it for text.
